HSRP — Hot Standby Router Protocol

A host can hold exactly one default gateway address, and it will never change it on its own. Buy two routers and one of them still becomes a single point of failure the moment you type an IP into a client. First-hop redundancy protocols fix that by inventing a router that doesn't exist — a virtual IP and a virtual MAC that two boxes take turns wearing. This guide covers HSRP end to end, plus VRRP and GLBP, the tracking that keeps it honest, and every way it breaks in the field. Press / to search.

FoundationsPROBLEM

The Single Default Gateway Problem

Redundancy at Layer 3 is easy. Routers run OSPF or floating statics and reroute around a dead link in seconds without anyone noticing. Redundancy at the very first hop — the step where a PC hands its packet to a router — is not, because that step isn't routed at all. It's a fixed value in a config box.

A host's logic is brutally simple: if the destination is on my subnet, ARP for it directly; otherwise send it to my default gateway. That gateway is one IP address. There is no metric, no second choice, no failover list. If the box holding that address dies, the host keeps ARPing into the void.

Why the obvious workarounds fail

  • Split the hosts manually — half the PCs point at .2, half at .3. It balances load, but a single router failure still blacks out exactly 50% of users, and recovery means touching every affected machine.
  • Push a new gateway by script — assumes the management platform can reach a host whose only path off the subnet is the dead router. It usually can't.
  • Rely on ICMP redirects or proxy ARP — fragile, slow, disabled in most hardened builds, and utterly dependent on the host stack behaving.
  • Just use DHCP option 3 — DHCP hands out one gateway, and the client won't ask for a new one until its lease renews. A client holding a 24-hour lease is offline for up to 24 hours.

You've booked one taxi to the airport and written the driver's plate number on your hand. The driver breaks down on the way. There are five other cabs idling on your street, but you're not looking for cabs — you're looking for that plate. Standing on the kerb reciting a number at empty cars is exactly what a PC does when its gateway dies.

The failure is silent from the switch's point of view. Every link light is green, spanning tree is stable, the switch has no errors. Nothing in Layer 1 or Layer 2 is wrong. The break is purely that a host is asking for an IP nobody owns any more.

FoundationsSOLUTION

What an FHRP Actually Does

A First-Hop Redundancy Protocol (FHRP) doesn't teach hosts anything new. Hosts stay dumb — that's the whole design goal. Instead, the routers cooperate to invent a router that doesn't physically exist and hand it out as the gateway.

That phantom router owns two things, and both matter equally:

ElementWhat it isWhy it's needed
Virtual IP (VIP)An unused address in the subnet, e.g. 10.10.10.1, configured identically on both routers.This is what every host types as its default gateway. It never changes, so hosts never need to.
Virtual MAC (vMAC)A protocol-derived MAC, e.g. 0000.0c07.ac01 for HSRPv1 group 1.Hosts cache MAC addresses, not IPs. Without a shared vMAC, every host would need to re-ARP after failover — and ARP caches live for minutes.

The failover, step by step

  1. Steady state — R1 is Active. It answers ARP requests for 10.10.10.1 with the vMAC, and it sources forwarded frames from that vMAC so SW1 learns it on R1's port.
  2. R1 dies — hellos stop arriving at R2.
  3. Hold timer expires — after 10 seconds of silence (default), R2 declares R1 gone and transitions to Active.
  4. R2 assumes the identity — it starts answering ARP for the VIP and sends a gratuitous ARP sourced from the vMAC, which makes SW1 re-learn that MAC on R2's port.
  5. Hosts notice nothing — their ARP cache still says "10.10.10.1 is at 0000.0c07.ac01," and that statement is still true. Not one host reconfigures anything.

Think of a company's support@ mailbox. Customers never email Priya or Dave directly, because people take holidays and leave jobs. They email the shared address. Priya is the one actually reading it today; if she's out, Dave picks it up and the customer never learns a handover happened. The VIP is the mailbox address, the vMAC is the mailbox itself, and Active/Standby is who's got it open.

The vMAC is the half people forget. If failover only moved the IP, every host would sit sending frames to a dead router's MAC until its ARP cache aged out — and ARP entries commonly persist for minutes to hours depending on the host stack (Cisco IOS defaults to a four-hour ARP timeout). Moving the MAC too is what makes the switchover invisible.

HSRP and VRRP are a duty roster: one person on shift, one on call, and the on-call person is being paid to do nothing. GLBP is a proper rota where everyone takes calls and the switchboard spreads them around. GLBP gets you more out of the hardware; HSRP and VRRP get you a simpler thing to troubleshoot at 3 a.m.

Exam trap: VRRP is not UDP. HSRP and GLBP ride inside UDP datagrams; VRRP rides directly on IP as protocol number 112. If someone hands you an ACL blocking "UDP 112" as the cause of a VRRP outage, the ACL is a red herring.

Cisco's own guidance for years has been "use HSRP unless something forces your hand." VRRP when the peer is a Juniper, Arista, Fortinet or HPE box. GLBP only when a single subnet genuinely needs more forwarding capacity than one router provides — and be aware GLBP is largely a legacy feature now, absent from most modern Cisco platforms and dropped entirely on Nexus.

HSRP at a Glance — Numbers You Must Know

HSRP (Hot Standby Router Protocol) is Cisco's own FHRP, documented in the informational RFC 2281. A set of routers on one segment form an HSRP group, elect one Active router, one Standby, and leave any others in Listen. Only the Active forwards traffic sent to the virtual gateway.

Facts that show up in every exam and every ticket

  • Priority range 0–255, default 100. Higher wins.
  • Tie-break on equal priority: highest interface IP address wins.
  • Preemption is off by default. This surprises people more than any other HSRP behaviour.
  • Hellos are sourced from the router's real interface IP, never the VIP, with TTL 1 so they can't leave the segment.
  • The VIP must not equal any physical interface address in the group — unlike VRRP, HSRP has no "address owner" concept.
  • Group numbers, VIP, version and authentication must match on every router in the group. Priority and preempt deliberately differ.

Three HSRP message types exist: Hello (I'm here, here's my priority and state), Coup (I'm taking over — sent by a preempting router), and Resign (I'm standing down — sent by an Active router being shut down gracefully). A clean shutdown sends a Resign and fails over in well under a second; a yanked power cable doesn't, and costs you the full 10-second hold timer.

FoundationsKEY MECHANIC

How ARP Makes Failover Invisible

This is the mechanic that makes the whole protocol work, and it's the one most study notes skip. Follow the MAC address, not the IP.

PC1 resolving its gateway — before any failure
C:\> arp -a
  Internet Address      Physical Address      Type
  10.10.10.1            00-00-0c-07-ac-01     dynamic   <- the VIRTUAL mac, not R1's burned-in one
  10.10.10.11           50-9a-4c-c5-c3-ad     dynamic

SW1 — where that virtual MAC currently lives
SW1# show mac address-table | include 0c07.ac01
  10    0000.0c07.ac01    DYNAMIC     Gi0/1        <- R1's port, because R1 is Active

...R1 is powered off. Ten seconds later, on the same switch:
SW1# show mac address-table | include 0c07.ac01
  10    0000.0c07.ac01    DYNAMIC     Gi0/2        <- moved to R2's port

PC1's ARP cache, unchanged and still correct:
C:\> arp -a
  10.10.10.1            00-00-0c-07-ac-01     dynamic   <- never had to change

What actually moved

  1. The host's ARP cache did not move. IP 10.10.10.1 still maps to vMAC 0000.0c07.ac01. That binding is permanently true.
  2. The switch's MAC table moved. The vMAC was learned on Gi0/1, and after failover the same MAC is learned on Gi0/2.
  3. What triggered the move: the new Active router sends a gratuitous ARP with the vMAC as the source, and afterwards sources all forwarded frames from that vMAC. The switch re-learns the entry from ordinary flooding and learning — no special protocol involved.

A shop keeps trading under the same name and the same street address after new owners take over. Customers keep the address in their contacts and keep turning up. The postie updates his round because the mail now goes through a different door round the back. Nobody reprints a single business card.

You will see the vMAC "flap" between two switch ports in logs during a failover. On the switch this is normal and expected — that is the failover. What is not normal is that flap repeating every few seconds: that means HSRP itself is unstable, not that the switch is confused. Chase the HSRP state changes, not the MAC-move message.

TTL 1 is a security feature and a diagnostic. HSRP hellos are engineered so they physically cannot be routed off the local segment. If you ever see HSRP hellos from an unexpected source, they came in over Layer 2 — a mis-patched trunk, a stretched VLAN, or something bridged that shouldn't be.

A hello sourced from the real IP but with the virtual MAC in the Ethernet header trips people up in packet captures. That's correct behaviour for the Active router — it deliberately keeps the vMAC alive in every switch's forwarding table between user frames.

Protocol MechanicsELECTION

Priority, Election and the Tie-Break

Two rules decide who forwards. That's the entire election.

The asterisk that matters

The election decides who should be Active. It does not decide who is. HSRP is deliberately biased toward stability: a router already in the Active state keeps the role even when a higher-priority router appears. The newcomer sits in Standby, quietly better and completely unused, until you configure preempt.

R1 configured to win, R2 as backup
R1(config-if)# standby 1 priority 110     above the default 100 → R1 should be Active
R2(config-if)# standby 1 priority 90      below default → R2 should be Standby

Verify who actually won — and why
R1# show standby GigabitEthernet0/1 1 | include Priority|State|Standby router
  State is Active
  Standby router is 10.10.10.3, priority 90 (expires in 8.192 sec)
  Priority 110 (configured 110)
Priority valueMeaning in practice
0A router whose priority has been decremented to zero by tracking. It gives up the Active role immediately and cannot win an election.
1–99Deliberately below default — a router you want as backup even against an unconfigured peer.
100Factory default. If both routers show 100, nobody configured priority and the winner is an accident of addressing.
101–200The normal working range for a preferred Active. Leave room below for tracking decrements.
255Maximum. Nothing can decrement it far enough to matter in a small design, which is usually the wrong choice — you want tracking to be able to move it.

Design habit: set priorities in gaps of 20 — 110 and 90, not 101 and 100. Tracking decrements default to 10, and you want a decrement to actually cross the peer's value. Priority 101 vs 100 with a decrement of 10 lands you at 91, which does cross — but 101 vs 100 with a decrement of 1 does not, and that's the config that quietly never fails over.

Protocol MechanicsGOTCHA

Preemption — The Single Most Missed Command

standby <group> preempt gives a router permission to take the Active role from a peer whose priority is lower than its own. Without it, HSRP's rule is simply "whoever got there first stays."

ScenarioWithout preemptWith preempt
Both routers boot togetherHighest priority wins — no difference.Highest priority wins.
R1 (pri 110) reboots, R2 takes over, R1 returnsR2 stays Active. R1 sits in Standby indefinitely.R1 sends a Coup and reclaims Active.
R1 is added later to an existing groupR1 goes to Standby regardless of its priority.R1 takes over immediately.
Tracking drops the Active's priority below the Standby'sNothing happens. Tracking is useless.The Standby takes over — preempt must be on the Standby.
The four lines that make a deterministic HSRP pair
R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/1
R1(config-if)# standby 1 ip 10.10.10.1
R1(config-if)# standby 1 priority 110
R1(config-if)# standby 1 preempt          R1 reclaims Active after any recovery

R2(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/1
R2(config-if)# standby 1 ip 10.10.10.1
R2(config-if)# standby 1 priority 90
R2(config-if)# standby 1 preempt          REQUIRED if you ever want tracking to fail traffic over to R2

The tracking trap, stated plainly: tracking lowers the Active router's priority, but a router never surrenders the Active role on its own. The Standby is the one that has to grab it — and it can only do that if preempt is configured on the Standby. A tracking config with preempt only on the primary router looks perfect in show run and does absolutely nothing when the uplink fails.

Preempt delay — the fix for "it failed over into a black hole"

A router that has just rebooted has interfaces up and HSRP running long before OSPF has adjacencies or BGP has converged. If it preempts instantly, it becomes Active with an empty routing table and drops everything for 30–60 seconds.

R1(config-if)# standby 1 preempt delay minimum 120
wait 120 s after HSRP starts on this interface before preempting

R1(config-if)# standby 1 preempt delay reload 180
use a longer delay specifically after a device reload — the slowest case

R1(config-if)# standby 1 preempt delay sync 100
wait for IP redundancy clients to sync, up to 100 s, then preempt

The manager back from sick leave. Without preempt, she comes back and finds her deputy still running everything — and just accepts it, forever. With preempt, she takes her desk back the moment she walks in. With preempt delay, she takes it back only after she's read her email and knows what's going on — which is the version you actually want, because a manager who resumes command while still clueless does more damage than the deputy ever would.

Is leaving preempt off ever right? Occasionally. If both routers are truly identical in capacity and upstream path, "whoever is Active, stays Active" avoids a second outage when the primary recovers — and shields you from a flapping link preempting back and forth. But that's a conscious decision, and it should be documented. Preempt being off by accident is the far more common story.

Protocol MechanicsTIMERS

Hello and Hold Timers — Tuning Convergence

Defaults are hello 3 seconds, hold 10 seconds. Hold should always be roughly 3× hello so a single lost packet doesn't trigger a failover. Ten seconds of blackhole is a long time for a voice call or a TCP session, so tuning is common.

Second-granularity — supported by both HSRP versions
R1(config-if)# standby 1 timers 1 3
                          ^ ^
                     hello | hold  →  failover in ~3 s worst case

Sub-second — HSRPv2 recommended, msec keyword required
R1(config-if)# standby version 2
R1(config-if)# standby 1 timers msec 200 msec 750
hello 200 ms, hold 750 ms  →  failover under a second
Hello / HoldWorst-case outageWhere it fits
3 s / 10 s (default)~10 sGeneral data. Survives transient loss well. Too slow for voice.
1 s / 3 s~3 sThe common production compromise. Safe on almost any platform.
msec 200 / msec 750<1 sVoice and trading floors. Verify the platform processes HSRP in hardware, not the CPU.
msec 50 / msec 200~200 msAggressive. On a busy or software-forwarding platform this causes outages rather than preventing them.

Sub-second timers are a load decision, not a config decision. On a router where HSRP is processed in software, a CPU spike from a routing flap or an SNMP walk delays hello transmission — and with a 200 ms hold timer the group fails over because the box was busy, not because anything broke. That failover generates more CPU load. Aggressive timers are how a small problem becomes a self-sustaining outage.

If standby timers is not configured locally, a non-Active router learns the timers from the Active router's hellos. That's why changing timers on only one router often appears to work — until that router isn't the Active one any more. Configure timers on every router in the group.

Hold time is how long you wait for a colleague to answer before assuming they've left the building. Wait too long and work stops for no reason. Assume they're gone after two seconds of silence and you'll be constantly reassigning their tasks while they're just at the printer.

The classic exam trap: standby version 2 must be applied on every router in the group, on every relevant interface. A v1 router and a v2 router cannot parse each other's hellos, so each concludes it's alone and both go Active. You get a duplicate-IP situation with an unstable virtual MAC, and the running configs look correct at a glance. Also note that changing the version changes the virtual MAC, so hosts and switches have to re-learn — expect a brief interruption.

Why v2 moved off 224.0.0.2: that address means "all routers on this subnet," so every router had to punt every HSRP packet to the CPU just to work out it wasn't interested. 224.0.0.102 is HSRP's own address, which lets uninvolved routers ignore it cheaply. If you need groups above 255 — one per VLAN in a large campus, for instance — v2 isn't optional.

IPv6 hosts learn a gateway from Router Advertisements, so first-hop redundancy is less about "the address is hardcoded" and more about controlling which router wins and how fast it switches. HSRP for IPv6 requires version 2.

The IPv6 virtual address is link-local, not global — which confuses people coming from IPv4. Hosts always use a link-local address as their default gateway in IPv6, so that's the one that has to be redundant. The global prefix comes from the RA and doesn't need to move.

IPv4 gateways are like a phone number you write down and keep forever. IPv6 gateways are like a receptionist who calls out "I'm here, I'm your way out" every few seconds. Redundancy in IPv4 means keeping the number valid; in IPv6 it means making sure somebody keeps making the announcement.

Base ConfigurationTOPOLOGY

The Reference Topology

Every config in this guide is built on this lab. Interface names, IPs and VLANs stay consistent throughout, so you can lift any block straight into a rack.

The SW1–SW2 trunk is load-bearing, and it's the piece lab diagrams most often omit. R1 and R2 are not directly cabled on the LAN side — their hellos travel R1 → SW1 → trunk → SW2 → R2. Break that trunk, or prune VLAN 10 off it, and both routers instantly go Active while every link light stays green.

Convention worth adopting: make the HSRP group number equal the VLAN ID. Group 10 on VLAN 10, group 20 on VLAN 20. It costs nothing, makes show standby brief readable at a glance, and means the virtual MAC's last byte tells you the VLAN. With HSRPv2's 4096 groups you never run out.

Two mistakes cause most "HSRP won't come up" tickets, and both look fine in show run:
1. The VIP is inside a host's DHCP range or already assigned to a real device. HSRP won't stop you; you just get an address conflict. Exclude the VIP and both real router addresses from DHCP.
2. The VIP is set to one of the routers' own interface addresses. HSRP forbids this and rejects it — unlike VRRP, which allows it and calls it the address owner.

standby 10 name VLAN10-GW looks like decoration but earns its keep. It appears in syslog state-change messages, so a 2 a.m. log line reads "VLAN10-GW went Active" instead of a bare interface and group number. When you're managing forty groups across a campus, that's the difference between a diagnosis and a lookup.

The three prerequisites people forget

  1. ip routing must be enabled. Without it the switch has SVIs with addresses but no routing table, so HSRP comes up perfectly and the gateway forwards nothing.
  2. The VLAN must exist and be active. An SVI stays down/down until the VLAN exists in the database and at least one access port in that VLAN is up (or the VLAN is allowed on an up trunk). A down SVI means HSRP stuck in Initial.
  3. The VLAN must be allowed on the interconnect trunk. HSRP hellos for VLAN 20 travel tagged as VLAN 20. Prune 20 off the trunk and both switches go Active for group 20 while VLAN 10 stays perfectly healthy — which is exactly the kind of partial failure that wastes an afternoon.

Align HSRP with spanning tree. If DSW1 is the HSRP Active for VLAN 10 but DSW2 is the STP root for VLAN 10, every frame from a host takes a detour across the interconnect to reach its own gateway. It works, and it wastes half your interconnect bandwidth. Whoever is HSRP Active for a VLAN should be the STP root for that VLAN.

TrackingTHE BLACK HOLE

Why HSRP Alone Isn't Enough

HSRP watches exactly one thing: whether hellos keep arriving on the LAN interface. It has no idea whether the Active router can still reach the internet. That gap produces the nastiest FHRP failure there is — everything looks healthy and nothing works.

A receptionist who's still cheerfully at the desk taking your parcels — but the courier stopped collecting from the building this morning and nobody told her. She's present, responsive, and doing her job perfectly. Your parcels are going nowhere. The problem isn't that the desk is unstaffed, it's that nobody is checking whether the van still comes.

Tracking closes the gap. It ties HSRP priority to the health of something else — an uplink interface, a route in the table, or the reachability of a real destination. When that thing fails, the priority drops, and the peer takes over.

Users will tell you "the gateway is up, I can ping it." They're right, and it's irrelevant. Pinging the VIP proves the Active router is alive — it proves nothing about whether it can forward. When someone reports "internet down but the gateway pings," check the Active router's uplink before anything else.

The original mechanism: watch an interface's line protocol and decrement priority when it goes down. Simple, still supported, still on the exam.

The decrement must be big enough to actually cross the peer's priority. This exact config is written into production networks constantly, tests clean in show run, and fails silently the one night it matters. Always model the arithmetic, then shut the uplink in a maintenance window and watch it move.

Interface tracking only watches line protocol. If the WAN interface stays up but the provider's next hop is unreachable — a very common failure with an Ethernet handoff into a provider switch — line protocol never drops and tracking never fires. That's what enhanced object tracking is for.

TrackingMODERN

Enhanced Object Tracking

Instead of hard-wiring HSRP to an interface, define a tracked object and point HSRP at it. The object can be an interface, a route, an IP SLA probe, or a boolean combination of several — and the same object can drive HSRP, VRRP, GLBP and static routes at once.

--- Object 1: is the WAN interface's line protocol up? ---
R1(config)# track 1 interface GigabitEthernet0/0 line-protocol

--- Object 2: is a default route actually IN the routing table? ---
R1(config)# track 2 ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 reachability

--- Object 3: can we actually REACH something out there? ---
R1(config)# ip sla 1
R1(config-ip-sla)# icmp-echo 8.8.8.8 source-interface GigabitEthernet0/0
R1(config-ip-sla)# frequency 5
R1(config-ip-sla)# exit
R1(config)# ip sla schedule 1 life forever start-time now
R1(config)# track 3 ip sla 1 reachability

--- Object 10: require BOTH the interface AND real reachability ---
R1(config)# track 10 list boolean and
R1(config-track)# object 1
R1(config-track)# object 3
R1(config-track)# delay down 10 up 30     damping: fail fast, recover slowly

Security & DesignHARDENING

HSRP Authentication

By default HSRP hellos carry the plain-text string cisco and any device on the segment can join the group, claim priority 255, and become the gateway for everyone. Authentication is one line and there's no reason to skip it.

--- Plain text: stops typos and accidents, not attackers ---
R1(config-if)# standby 10 authentication text MYKEY01
the string travels in clear in every hello — visible in any capture

--- MD5 with an inline key: the practical minimum ---
R1(config-if)# standby 10 authentication md5 key-string MySecret123

--- MD5 with a key chain: supports rotation without an outage ---
R1(config)# key chain HSRP-KEYS
R1(config-keychain)# key 1
R1(config-keychain-key)# key-string MySecret123
R1(config-keychain-key)# accept-lifetime 00:00:00 Jan 1 2026 infinite
R1(config-keychain-key)# send-lifetime 00:00:00 Jan 1 2026 infinite
R1(config)# interface GigabitEthernet0/1
R1(config-if)# standby 10 authentication md5 key-chain HSRP-KEYS
ModeProtects againstVerdict
None (default "cisco")Nothing.Anyone on the VLAN can hijack the gateway.
Plain textMisconfiguration, a lab device plugged into production.Better than nothing. Readable in one packet capture.
MD5 key-stringUnauthenticated devices joining the group.The sensible default.
MD5 key chainSame, plus lets you rotate keys with overlapping lifetimes.Best practice where key rotation is a requirement.

Change authentication on both routers within the same maintenance window, and expect a blip. The moment the keys differ, each router discards the other's hellos, concludes it's alone, and goes Active. You get a duplicate-IP condition that lasts exactly as long as your mismatch does. If you're rotating keys on a live pair, use a key chain with overlapping accept-lifetimes so both keys are valid during the change.

An authentication mismatch and a blocked multicast path produce the identical symptom — both routers Active, neither seeing the other. debug standby errors distinguishes them instantly: an auth failure logs an explicit authentication mismatch, whereas a blocked path logs nothing at all because no packet ever arrives.

Security & DesignATTACK

HSRP Hijacking — and How to Stop It

The attack is trivial. A host on the VLAN sends HSRP hellos for the right group with priority 255 and preempt. It wins the election, becomes the gateway, and every packet leaving the subnet flows through it. Classic man-in-the-middle, with no ARP spoofing required.

What makes it possible

  • HSRP hellos are multicast to a well-known address — anything on the VLAN can hear the group number and VIP.
  • The default authentication string is cisco, publicly documented and universally left in place.
  • Priority 255 beats anything. There's no upper defence inside the protocol itself.
  • Every access port in the VLAN is, from HSRP's point of view, as trustworthy as the uplink to the peer router.
Layered mitigation — do all four, not one

--- 1. Authenticate. Attackers can't forge what they can't hash. ---
R1(config-if)# standby 10 authentication md5 key-string MySecret123

--- 2. Block HSRP from arriving on ACCESS ports (it only belongs on uplinks) ---
SW1(config)# ip access-list extended NO-HSRP-FROM-USERS
SW1(config-ext-nacl)# deny udp any host 224.0.0.102 eq 1985   HSRPv2
SW1(config-ext-nacl)# deny udp any host 224.0.0.2   eq 1985   HSRPv1
SW1(config-ext-nacl)# permit ip any any
SW1(config)# interface range GigabitEthernet1/0/1 - 20
SW1(config-if-range)# ip access-group NO-HSRP-FROM-USERS in

--- 3. Storm/port hygiene on access ports ---
SW1(config-if-range)# switchport mode access          never let a user port negotiate a trunk
SW1(config-if-range)# switchport nonegotiate
SW1(config-if-range)# spanning-tree bpduguard enable

--- 4. Alarm on state changes so a hijack is visible, not silent ---
R1(config)# logging host 10.10.20.50
R1(config)# snmp-server enable traps hsrp

A courier turns up in a convincing uniform, stands by the loading bay, and offers to take everything outbound. Staff hand over parcels without a second thought, because nobody at the bay was ever asked to check ID. Authentication is the ID badge. The port ACL is the rule that says deliveries only happen at the loading bay, never at the front desk.

Do not apply that HSRP-blocking ACL to a router-facing or inter-switch trunk port. The hellos must flow between the routers — filter them there and you've performed the outage yourself. Access ports only, and check what's plugged in before you apply it to a range.

The same attack shape applies to VRRP (protocol 112 to 224.0.0.18, priority 255 = address owner) and GLBP (UDP 3222 to 224.0.0.102, where winning the AVG role lets you hand out your own MAC to every host). GLBP is arguably worse, because a hijacked AVG can spread traffic in ways that look like normal load balancing.

Security & DesignCHECKLIST

Design Checklist

  1. Pick the version and set it everywhere. Use v2 for new builds. Mixed versions in one group means both routers go Active.
  2. Make group number = VLAN ID. Free readability in every show command and every log line.
  3. Set priority explicitly on both routers, with a gap of at least 20. Two routers at the default 100 is an accident, not a design.
  4. Configure preempt on every router in every group. Including the backup — that's what makes tracking work.
  5. Add preempt delay minimum 120. Stops a freshly rebooted router taking over before its routing table exists.
  6. Track the uplink, and check the arithmetic. The decrement must genuinely cross the peer's priority, not tie with it.
  7. Prefer object tracking over interface tracking. An IP SLA probe catches the "link up, path dead" failure that line-protocol tracking misses entirely.
  8. Authenticate with MD5. One line, and it removes the single easiest gateway hijack there is.
  9. Align HSRP Active with the STP root per VLAN. Otherwise every frame crosses the interconnect for nothing.
  10. Exclude the VIP and both real router IPs from DHCP. An address conflict on the gateway is a memorable afternoon.
  11. Log and trap state changes. An HSRP failover that nobody noticed means you're now running without redundancy and don't know it.
  12. Test failover before you sign the change off. Shut the tracked uplink, watch a continuous ping, confirm the state moves. A design that has never been tested has never worked.

Sizing rule: each router must be able to carry 100% of the load, not 50%. The whole point is that one of them can take everything. If your load balancing is what keeps you under capacity, then a single failure produces congestion instead of redundancy — and you'll spend the outage explaining why "the failover worked" and the network was still unusable.

Watch out for HSRP under stateful firewalls or NAT. If both routers do NAT or hold connection state, a failover moves the gateway but not the state table, so every established session breaks even though HSRP converged perfectly. Firewalls need their own HA mechanism with state sync — HSRP is not a substitute for it.

VRRP (Virtual Router Redundancy Protocol) does the same job as HSRP with the same architecture, but it's an IETF standard — RFC 3768 for v2 and RFC 5798 for v3 — so a Cisco router can form a group with a Juniper, Arista, Fortinet, HPE or Palo Alto device.

The address-owner concept is genuinely different. In VRRP you can set the VIP to be a router's actual interface address. It's tidy in small setups, but it also means you can't fail that priority away with tracking. Most enterprise designs deliberately use a VIP that belongs to nobody.

GLBPACTIVE/ACTIVE

GLBP — Load Balancing Inside One Group

GLBP (Gateway Load Balancing Protocol) is Cisco's answer to the idle-router problem. One virtual IP, but several virtual MACs — and the trick is that hosts get different answers when they ARP for the gateway.

Roles

RoleCountJob
AVG — Active Virtual GatewayOne per groupAnswers every ARP for the VIP, assigns virtual MACs to forwarders, and decides which host gets which MAC. Elected by highest priority; tie broken by highest IP.
AVF — Active Virtual ForwarderUp to fourOwns one virtual MAC and forwards the traffic sent to it. The AVG is normally also an AVF.
Standby AVGOneTakes over the AVG role if it fails.
Secondary VFPer forwarderBacks up another router's virtual MAC. If an AVF dies, another router adopts its vMAC so hosts holding it keep working.

A restaurant with one phone number and four waiters. Every booking call comes to the same number, and the maître d' answering it quietly assigns you to a different waiter each time. You never picked a waiter and you never knew there were four. If one waiter walks out mid-shift, another silently takes over his tables — including yours.

GLBP's genuinely clever bit is secondary virtual forwarders. If R2 dies, the hosts that were handed R2's vMAC don't have to re-ARP — R1 simply starts answering for R2's virtual MAC as well, and keeps doing so until those hosts naturally re-ARP and get rebalanced. Failover is invisible for the same reason HSRP's is: the MAC moves, not the host config.

Priority and weighting are two separate mechanisms and people conflate them constantly. priority only decides who becomes the AVG — the router answering ARPs. weighting decides whether a router remains a forwarder at all. Track your uplink with weighting, not priority: a router that has lost its WAN circuit should stop forwarding, and lowering its AVG priority achieves nothing useful because the AVG role is just an ARP responder.

Reality check on GLBP. It's Cisco-only, it's been removed from Nexus entirely, and it's absent or deprecated on much modern Catalyst hardware. Most designs that once reached for GLBP now use a stacked or virtualised distribution pair — StackWise Virtual, VSS, vPC — where the two switches present as one and load balancing falls out of port-channel hashing for free. Know GLBP for the exam and for legacy networks; don't design new ones around it.

Debugging and Proving Failover Works

Typical output during a real failover
*Aug 23 14:22:05: HSRP: Gi0/1 Grp 10 Standby: c/Active timer expired (10.10.10.2)
*Aug 23 14:22:05: HSRP: Gi0/1 Grp 10 Standby -> Active
*Aug 23 14:22:05: HSRP: Gi0/1 Grp 10 Added 10.10.10.1 to ARP (0000.0c9f.f00a)
*Aug 23 14:22:05: %HSRP-5-STATECHANGE: GigabitEthernet0/1 Grp 10 state Standby -> Active

Never run debug standby packets on a production distribution switch with dozens of groups. Every group generates a hello every three seconds, all of it goes to the console, and console output is process-switched at high priority. The debug itself can drive CPU high enough to delay real hellos — and cause the very failover you were investigating. debug standby errors answers most questions and costs almost nothing.

The failover test — the only proof that counts

  1. Baseline. Capture show standby brief from both routers. Confirm the Active/Standby split matches the design and every group shows P.
  2. Start a continuous ping from a real host to something beyond the gateway — not to the gateway itself. ping -t 8.8.8.8 on Windows, ping 8.8.8.8 on anything else. Pinging the VIP would keep succeeding through a black hole and tell you nothing.
  3. Trigger the failure you actually care about. Shut the tracked uplink, not the LAN interface. Anyone can prove HSRP survives a dead router; the interesting question is whether it survives a dead circuit.
  4. Count the lost pings. Roughly 3–4 lost at default timers, under 1 with tuned timers. Zero lost usually means the failover didn't actually happen — check state, don't celebrate.
  5. Confirm the state moved on both routers, and that the switch's MAC table now shows the vMAC on the new port.
  6. Restore the uplink and confirm it fails back — this is where a missing preempt or an undersized decrement finally shows itself.
  7. Repeat in the other direction. A pair that fails over cleanly one way and hangs the other way is a very common and very embarrassing finding.

A fire drill you only ever run in one direction is a fire drill for one exit. Everyone files out of the front and congratulates themselves, and nobody has checked whether the back door opens. Test both ways, in a window, before you need it.

Scenario — Both Routers Active

Symptom: Intermittent connectivity across the whole VLAN. Some flows work, some don't, and it changes minute to minute. Both routers report State is Active.

The confirming output — note what's MISSING
R1# show standby GigabitEthernet0/1 10
  State is Active
  Active router is local

  Priority 110 (configured 110)

  Standby router is unknown

What's happening: two routers are answering ARP for the same IP with the same virtual MAC. Whichever reply a host got last determines where its traffic goes, and switches see the vMAC bouncing between two ports. The result looks random because it is.

Work through these in order — most common first

  1. Is the VLAN allowed and active on the trunk between the switches? This is the number-one cause by a wide margin. show interfaces trunk — check the allowed and active line, not just allowed. Adding a new VLAN and forgetting the trunk's allowed list creates this exact fault.
  2. Version mismatch. show standby | include version on both. v1 and v2 cannot parse each other's hellos.
  3. Authentication mismatch. Compare the Authentication line on each. Different keys means hellos are silently discarded — same symptom as no path at all.
  4. Group number mismatch. R1 in group 10 and R2 in group 1 are two independent groups that happen to share a VIP.
  5. An ACL or filter dropping the multicast. Check for anything denying udp … 224.0.0.2 or 224.0.0.102 on the path, including a port ACL applied to a router-facing port by mistake.
  6. A physical or STP break. The Layer 2 path exists in the diagram but is blocking or down in reality. show spanning-tree vlan 10.

Scenario — Failover Happened, Users Still Down

Symptom: HSRP converged correctly. R2 is Active, its uplink is fine, its routing table is complete. Some hosts work, some don't, and the ones that don't have been down since the failover.

Where to look

  1. Switch MAC table stale. The new Active's gratuitous ARP was lost or filtered. The entry ages out on its own eventually, but that's five minutes of outage. clear mac address-table dynamic vlan 10 confirms the diagnosis instantly.
  2. Host ARP caches stale. Rare, since the vMAC didn't change — but a host with a static ARP entry for the gateway, or one that cached R1's real MAC because something was misconfigured with use-bia, will stay broken. arp -d * on the host proves it.
  3. Asymmetric return path. Traffic leaves via R2 but return traffic still arrives at R1, which no longer has a path back to the LAN. This is what the inter-router floating static exists to catch.
  4. Stateful device in the path. A firewall or NAT gateway that only had R1's sessions. Established connections break; new ones work. If "old sessions dead, new sessions fine" describes it, this is your answer.
  5. Port security or DAI on the access switch. A security feature that learned the topology as it was and is now dropping the new path's frames. Check show port-security and show ip arp inspection statistics.
Fast triage
SW1# show mac address-table address 0000.0c9f.f00a
SW1# clear mac address-table dynamic vlan 10      if this fixes it, gARP was the problem

Field ReferenceEXAM

Exam Traps and Interview Answers

  1. Preemption is OFF by default in HSRP and ON by default in VRRP. The most-tested single fact in the whole topic.
  2. HSRP: higher priority wins. Not lower. Contrast with STP bridge priority and OSPF cost, where lower wins — that contrast is exactly why it's asked.
  3. Tie-break is the highest interface IP, the real one, not the virtual one.
  4. VRRP uses IP protocol 112, not UDP. HSRP uses UDP 1985; GLBP uses UDP 3222.
  5. HSRPv1 = 224.0.0.2. HSRPv2 = 224.0.0.102. The v2 address is shared with GLBP.
  6. Virtual MAC decoding: 0000.0c07.ac0a is HSRPv1 group 10; 0000.0c9f.f00a is HSRPv2 group 10; 0000.5e00.010a is VRRP VRID 10. The last field is always the group in hex.
  7. Version is per-interface, and mismatched versions make both routers Active.
  8. The HSRP virtual IP can't be a real interface address. VRRP's can — that's the address owner, priority 255.
  9. Only HSRPv2 supports IPv6, and the virtual address is link-local.
  10. GLBP terminology: one AVG answering ARPs, up to four AVFs forwarding. Load balancing is round-robin, weighted or host-dependent.
  11. Tracking needs preempt on the router that will take over. The favourite trick question, and the favourite real-world outage.
  12. Default hold time is 10 s, so default worst-case failover is about 10 s — not instant.
  13. HSRPv1 has one Standby; extra routers sit in Listen. VRRP calls every non-Master router a Backup.

CCNA 200-301 scope: objective 3.5 asks you to describe the purpose, function and concepts of first hop redundancy protocols — the terminology, the virtual IP/MAC idea, and the differences between HSRP, VRRP and GLBP. Configuration isn't formally required at CCNA level, but it's fair game in interviews and it's the whole job in practice, which is why it's covered in full here. Full HSRP configuration and tuning is core ENCOR/CCNP material.

If an interviewer asks one FHRP question, it's usually "what happens when the primary router comes back?" The answer they're listening for is: nothing, unless preempt is configured — and then, ideally, that you'd add a preempt delay so it doesn't take over before its routing table is ready. That second half is what separates someone who read the book from someone who has been paged at 4 a.m.

  Active, the Active is healthy on the LAN, and every state is correct.

Quick RefresherELECTION

Election, preemption and the question behind the question

"Which router becomes Active?" is the opening question. The follow-up is almost always some version of "and what happens when the original one comes back?" — which is where preemption lives, and where most candidates lose the point.

Preemption, in the words that land

  • Priority says who deserves the role. Preempt says whether the role is allowed to move. Without preempt, whoever got there first keeps it — for years, if nothing restarts.
  • HSRP defaults to preempt OFF; VRRP defaults to ON. Say both halves; the contrast is the point of the question.
  • Tracking is useless without preempt on the peer. Lowering your own priority achieves nothing unless the other router is configured to act on it. This is the favourite trick question and also a real-world outage.
  • Always add a delay: preempt delay minimum 120. A router that has just rebooted has interfaces up before its routing table is complete — take the gateway role back too early and you black-hole traffic for the seconds it takes BGP or OSPF to converge.
R1(config-if)# standby 10 priority 110
R1(config-if)# standby 10 preempt delay minimum 120
R2(config-if)# standby 10 preempt
  The complete pattern. R1 is the intended primary, so it needs both a
  higher priority AND preempt to reclaim the role after a reboot.

  R2 needs preempt too — that is the half people forget. Without it,
  tracking on R1 can drop R1's priority to 10 and R2 will sit in Standby
  watching the black hole rather than taking over.

  Rule of thumb: preempt goes on BOTH routers. Priority goes on one.

The sentence that makes this stick: tracking is the router raising its hand to say it is no longer the best choice, and preempt is the other router being allowed to respond. Configure one without the other and you have built a system where a router can report a problem that nobody is permitted to act on.

Quick RefresherTRACKING

Tracking and the black hole — the concept that separates candidates

If an interviewer wants to find out whether you have run HSRP in production rather than read about it, this is the question they use. The concept is simple; the reason it matters is not obvious until you have seen it happen.

The problem in one sentence: HSRP watches the LAN interface it is configured on and nothing else — so if the Active router's WAN uplink dies, HSRP sees a perfectly healthy router, keeps it Active, and every host on the segment sends its traffic to a router that cannot forward it. The gateway answers ping. Nothing beyond it works. Nothing fails over and nothing is logged.

The fix, and the arithmetic

! ----- modern: enhanced object tracking (preferred) -----
R1(config)# track 1 interface GigabitEthernet0/0 line-protocol
R1(config)# track 2 ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 reachability
R1(config)# track 10 list boolean and
R1(config-track)#  object 1
R1(config-track)#  object 2
R1(config-track)#  delay down 10 up 30

R1(config-if)# standby 10 track 10 decrement 30
R2(config-if)# standby 10 preempt

  Why "ip route reachability" beats tracking the interface: a WAN link can
  be up at Layer 1 while the provider has lost everything behind it. Losing
  the default route is the failure you actually care about, and the boolean
  list lets you require both conditions.

  "delay down 10 up 30" damps a flapping circuit — it must be down for 10
  seconds before HSRP reacts, and up for 30 before the role returns.

! ----- legacy syntax, still common in the field -----
R1(config-if)# standby 10 track GigabitEthernet0/0 30
  Interface line-protocol only. Fine for a simple design, blind to the
  "link up, path dead" case above.

  The default decrement is 10, and on the extremely common 110/100 design
  that is exactly one short of working, because HSRP keeps the incumbent
  on a tie. Make the decrement comfortably LARGER than the priority gap —
  and if you are tracking several objects, make sure any single one is
  enough on its own.

Proving it works

R1# show standby | include Priority|Track
  Priority 80 (configured 110)
  Track object 10 state Down decrement 30
  Effective versus configured is the whole story. If these two are equal
  while the uplink is down, your tracking is not bound to the group.

R1# show track 1
  Tracked by:
    HSRP GigabitEthernet0/1 10
  The "Tracked by" section proves the binding exists. An object that is
  configured correctly but attached to nothing looks healthy everywhere
  else and protects you from nothing.

The answer that sounds senior: "HSRP only monitors the interface it lives on, so I track the uplink or preferably the default route, decrement by more than the priority gap, and make sure preempt is on the peer — otherwise the Active router keeps advertising a gateway it can't route through." That one sentence contains the problem, the mechanism, the arithmetic trap and the configuration trap. It is hard to say by accident.

The commands, and what each one buys you

If you are asked to configure HSRP on a whiteboard, this is the order to write it in. Each line earns its place, and being able to say why each is there matters more than reciting all of them.

interface GigabitEthernet0/1
 ip address 10.10.10.2 255.255.255.0
 standby version 2              groups 0–4095, IPv6 capable, distinct vMAC
 standby 10 ip 10.10.10.1       the virtual IP — the hosts' default gateway
 standby 10 priority 110        higher wins; only needed on the intended primary
 standby 10 preempt delay minimum 120
                                 reclaim the role, but not before routing converges
 standby 10 track 1 decrement 30
                                 stand down if the uplink or default route is gone
 standby 10 authentication md5 key-string MySecret123
                                 stops a rogue device claiming priority 255
 standby 10 name VLAN10-GW      cosmetic, but it makes every show output readable
 standby 10 timers msec 250 msec 750
                                 optional: ~750 ms failover instead of ~10 s

The minimum that works is TWO lines — "standby 10 ip" on both routers.
Everything else above is the difference between HSRP existing and HSRP
being trustworthy. If you are asked what the minimum is, say two lines
and then immediately say which additions you would never ship without:
preempt, tracking and authentication.

  The four things that must match on both routers before a group can even

The three sentences that answer the comparison question

  • HSRP and VRRP do the same job — one Active, one virtual IP, a standby waiting. The differences that matter in practice are that VRRP is an open standard, its preemption is on by default, and its virtual IP can legitimately be one of the routers' real addresses.
  • GLBP solves a different problem. HSRP wastes the standby router's capacity, and MHSRP works around that with multiple groups and split VLANs. GLBP does it inside one group: a single AVG answers every ARP but hands out different virtual MACs to different hosts, so traffic spreads across up to four forwarders without any per-VLAN design work.
  • MHSRP versus GLBP is the follow-up. MHSRP balances per VLAN and needs you to plan it; GLBP balances per host and needs nothing. MHSRP works with any vendor's equivalent; GLBP is Cisco-only.

The design point worth adding unprompted: whichever you pick, align the Active router with the Spanning Tree root for that VLAN. If HSRP is Active on one distribution switch while the STP root is the other, every packet crosses the interswitch link twice — the network works, and half its capacity goes to traffic that should never have been there.

Quick RefresherQ&A

Twelve questions you will actually be asked

Model answers kept to the length you should speak them — short, then one supporting detail. That shape reads as confidence rather than padding.

QuestionAnswer
What problem does HSRP solve?A host has one default gateway and cannot fail over on its own. HSRP gives routers a shared virtual IP and MAC so the host never has to know which router is alive.
How is the Active elected?Highest priority — default 100, higher wins. Ties go to the highest real interface IP, and on an exact tie the incumbent keeps the role.
How long does failover take?About 10 seconds on defaults, because the hold timer drives it. Tune to msec 250 msec 750 for sub-second.
Why is failover invisible to hosts?The virtual MAC moves with the role, so the host's ARP cache is still correct. The new Active sends a gratuitous ARP so switches relearn the port.
What is preemption and what is the default?It lets a higher-priority router take the role back. Off by default in HSRP, on in VRRP.
Why add a preempt delay?A rebooted router has interfaces up before its routing table is complete. Taking the gateway role back too early black-holes traffic.
What is tracking for?HSRP only watches its own interface. Tracking lowers priority when the uplink or default route fails, so the peer can take over instead of black-holing.
Tracking is configured but nothing fails over — why?Preempt is missing on the peer, or the decrement is too small to cross its priority. The default decrement of 10 exactly ties a 110/100 pair.
Both routers show Active — why?They cannot hear each other. Pruned VLAN, version mismatch, authentication mismatch, different group numbers, or an ACL.
HSRPv1 vs v2?v2 has groups 0–4095 instead of 0–255, a different multicast and virtual MAC, millisecond timers and IPv6 support. Version is per interface, and a mismatch makes both routers Active.
How do you load balance with HSRP?Multiple groups on the same subnet with the Active role split between routers — MHSRP. Or use GLBP, which balances inside a single group.
How would you secure it?MD5 key-string authentication, so a device sending priority 255 cannot take the gateway role. Combined with port security and control-plane hygiene on the access layer.

Two habits make these land better than the words alone. Answer the question before adding context — "highest priority" then the detail, not a two-minute wind-up. And when you do not know, say so and then say what you would run to find out: "I'd check show standby brief first" is a far stronger answer than a guess, and it is what the job actually involves.

Quick RefresherTRAPS

The traps — where confident candidates lose points

Statements that sound right, get repeated constantly, and are wrong. Getting one of these correct is worth more than a page of definitions.

Commonly saidActually
"Lower priority wins, like STP."Higher wins in HSRP. The contrast with STP and OSPF is exactly why it is asked.
"The tie-break is the virtual IP."The highest real interface address.
"Preempt is on by default."Off in HSRP, on in VRRP. The most-tested single fact in the topic.
"Tracking makes the peer take over."Tracking only lowers your priority. Preempt on the peer is what lets it act.
"Decrement 10 is fine."On a 110/100 pair it produces a tie, and the incumbent keeps the role.
"Failover takes 3 seconds — the hello time."The hold timer drives it. About 10 seconds on defaults.
"Hosts need to re-ARP after a failover."They do not — the virtual MAC moves with the role. That is the entire design.
"The gateway pings, so HSRP is fine."A black-holing Active router pings perfectly. Check show ip route 0.0.0.0 on it.
"Both Active means a split brain bug."It means the hellos are not arriving. Look at Layer 2 before you look at HSRP.
"VRRP is just HSRP renamed."Different transport (IP 112), different defaults, and its virtual IP may be a real address.
"GLBP is HSRP with load balancing."GLBP balances inside one group by handing out different vMACs. MHSRP balances between groups.
"HSRP handles IPv6."Only v2, and the virtual address is link-local.
"Version doesn't really matter."It is per interface, and a mismatch makes both routers Active.

If there is one line to carry into the room, make it this: HSRP does not make the network redundant — the second router already did that. HSRP makes the redundancy reachable by a host that can only hold one gateway address. Everything else in this guide is a detail hanging off that sentence, and an interviewer who hears it knows they are talking to someone who understands the protocol rather than someone who revised it.
